Microcontrollers

This project was completed as part of Introduction to Mechatronics and Robotics, a one-semester, lab-based course in which teams were given broad autonomy to design and build an autonomous robot for a competition. The project was developed under a strict budget of less than $50 per team member, requiring careful design tradeoffs and component selection.

The robot was designed to autonomously navigate a closed-loop course, traverse ramps and a gravel pit, avoid obstacles, and collect and sort colored dice. The system was built around modular subsystems, including a tank-tread drive system for reliable traction, a custom dice collection mechanism, and a servo-actuated sorting system. Most mechanical components were designed in SolidWorks and fabricated using a combination of laser-cut acrylic and 3D-printed PLA.

Control was distributed across multiple microcontrollers. An Arduino Mega handled real-time motor control and obstacle avoidance, while a Raspberry Pi processed vision data for dice detection and higher-level decision making. A late-stage shift from time-of-flight sensors to calibrated infrared sensors significantly improved responsiveness and reliability, reinforcing the importance of sensor selection and system-level tradeoffs in autonomous robotics.

CAD Assemblies

This project involved the reverse engineering and redesign of a consumer product to identify inefficiencies in manufacturing and assembly and implement targeted design-for-assembly (DFA) improvements. The work emphasized a structured workflow that moved from system-level understanding to part-level analysis, allowing design changes to be driven by measurable assembly and economic metrics rather than intuition alone.

The process began with a complete teardown and decomposition of the product into functional subsystems, supported by black box and glass box diagrams to map inputs, outputs, and internal energy transfer. Fishbone diagrams were used to organize parts and subassemblies, after which the entire product was reconstructed as a detailed 3D CAD model. A complete engineering drawing package was generated, including fully dimensioned drawings for every component, enabling clear communication of design intent and manufacturability.

>

Using this model, DFA metrics such as part count, part variety, assembly difficulty, and error risk were evaluated to identify the highest-impact opportunities for improvement. Design changes were then implemented to directly target these metrics, including error-proofing critical assemblies, simplifying the firing mechanism to reduce assembly steps and tolerance stack-up, and standardizing fasteners to reduce part variety and handling complexity. The resulting designs demonstrated clear improvements in assembly efficiency and projected manufacturing cost, illustrating how data-driven DFA analysis can guide effective mechanical design decisions.

Hologic Inc. contracted my senior design team through Design Center Colorado, an embedded consulting program at CU Boulder, to develop a prototype fluid management system for the next generation of the Fluent Pro system. Our team won both the judges Distinction in Design award and the People's Choice award at the 2026 CU Boulder Engineering Expo for our work on this project.

This device manages uterine distension during hysteroscopic surgical procedures, enabling surgeons to maintain a clear view of the surgical site while supporting precise and safe tissue resection.

The project has involved detailed research into fluid dynamics, control systems, and medical device design, with a focus on developing innovative solutions within the unique constraints of the medical device industry.

Autonomous Cup Pong Robot

$1,200

14 weeks

6
Motors

This project was completed as part of Mechatronics and Robotics 2, a one-semester, lab-based course centered on the design and construction of autonomous electromechanical systems. Working within a team of six and a total project budget of approximately $1,200, we developed an automated cup pong launcher capable of detecting cups and autonomously launching ping pong balls into them using computer vision and closed-loop motion control.

The system combined mechanical design, embedded systems, and machine vision into a two-axis articulated launcher platform. A Raspberry Pi 5 handled computer vision and high-level targeting logic using OpenCV-based cup detection, while a Raspberry Pi Pico controlled the real-time actuation hardware, including stepper motors, brushless flywheel motors, and servo-driven feeding mechanisms. Communication between the controllers was implemented over serial, while motor subsystems utilized both PWM and DSHOT protocols for precise control.

The launcher itself used dual TPU flywheels powered by repurposed drone motors to accelerate the balls, while a custom multi-stage feeding system ensured consistent loading conditions for repeatable trajectories. After multiple design iterations, the final feeder architecture combined a stepper-driven indexing system with a servo-actuated rack-and-pinion mechanism to improve firing consistency and eliminate common misfeed conditions. Mechanical components were designed in SolidWorks and fabricated using a combination of 3D-printed PLA, machined aluminum, and sheet metal structures.

A major focus of the project was iterative systems engineering and hardware refinement. Early PLA structural components proved insufficiently rigid under motor loads and vibration, leading to a redesign using 5052 aluminum sheet metal and machined 6061 bearing housings. Similarly, several sensing and feeding approaches were abandoned and redesigned during testing to improve reliability and repeatability, reinforcing the importance of subsystem integration, manufacturability, and design iteration in complex electromechanical systems.

Drill-Powered Bicycle

~$3,000
Budget

Welds

As part of a six-person team, I helped design and build a drill-powered bicycle using a custom welded steel frame and a go-kart–style front wheel steering system rather than a conventional bicycle fork and head tube. The steering geometry was implemented through a steering shaft and linkage, allowing for precise control while accommodating the structural constraints of the frame. Power was delivered to the rear wheel via a drivetrain driven by a handheld power drill mounted in pillow blocks, with mechanical controls that allowed one brake lever to actuate standard bicycle brakes and the other to modulate drill input.

The entire system was fully designed in CAD prior to fabrication, including the frame, steering mechanism, drivetrain, and mounting interfaces. The project followed a structured engineering workflow with design reviews at key milestones, where concepts were evaluated for structural integrity, manufacturability, and safety before being approved for fabrication. High-stress regions in the frame and drivetrain were identified through hand calculations and simplified analysis, informing material selection, joint design, and weld placement.

Following the design phase, the team transitioned into fabrication and assembly, which required learning and applying MIG welding techniques and translating CAD intent into a physically buildable system. The project emphasized end-to-end engineering: moving from concept and analysis through detailed design, fabrication, integration, and testing, while coordinating work across a large team under time and resource constraints.

Note on AI-Assisted Development

Much of this website was built using AI-assisted "vibe-coding" workflows. I want to acknowledge that while these tools are incredibly useful in reducing the barrier to entry for new developers in low-stakes contexts like this one, I would not consider myself a web developer, and would not rely on these tools as a substitute for real expertise and human judgment. Moreover, I want to acknowledge the very real environmental and economic costs currently associated with these tools.

This website was designed and built from scratch to reflect a specific vision: a clean, content-first portfolio with full control over structure, styling, and iteration. I experimented with website builders like Wix, but found the process limiting and difficult to work with. I am not a web developer, I'd never written a word of HTML before this, so I chose to develop this site locally using lightweight web technologies and an AI-assisted “vibe-coding” workflow. This approach allowed me to make precise, incremental changes to layout, content, and styling without fighting a platform’s constraints.

The site was created entirely with free tools and is hosted via GitHub Pages, enabling a simple and reproducible deployment pipeline. Working locally allowed rapid experimentation and version control, while GitHub-based hosting made publishing updates effectively instantaneous. Throughout development, AI tools were used to accelerate front-end scaffolding and iteration, while all structural decisions, content organization, and final code edits were made intentionally and reviewed manually.

Beyond the technical implementation, this project emphasizes communication and design judgment: translating complex engineering work into clear written narratives, establishing visual hierarchy across projects, and maintaining consistency as the site evolves. The result is a flexible, low-overhead platform that I can continuously refine and extend, and a demonstration of how modern tooling can be leveraged to move efficiently from concept to a polished, maintainable product.
